Asking backwards

What do I have to believe?

Excel answers “what if?” An acquisitions team asks it the other way round: what's the most we can pay, and what has to be true for it? Today that is Goal Seek, one question at a time, or a data table rebuilt for every new question. Here the model answers it backwards, and every point below is one of its runs: 101 runs in 6 seconds for one lever, thousands for twenty.

      Believe together

      The twenty are drawn independently, on purpose: to say some move together, believe them together. A soft market believes occupancy, both rents and rent growth on their weak side; a labor squeeze, wage and cost growth on theirs; a capital-markets shock, exit cap and interest rate on theirs.       How it got here

      Behind every number above is one question: how do you get AI to read a spreadsheet?

      July 2024. A paper went by on my feed.

      Microsoft Research had my problem: a spreadsheet is too big, and too much a grid, for a language model to read.

      Their answer: compress it until it fits.

      I thought: I can do this.

      How much of the model does one number need?

      Select GP IRR. Click Trace Precedents: one cell.

      Click again: 386 more.

      Keep clicking.

      By the fifteenth click: 31,485 formulas on five sheets. 60% of the workbook, for one number.

      In Excel, the screen would be solid arrows.

      That is what “explain this IRR” costs. Every time you ask.

      How do you get AI to read a spreadsheet?

      You don’t.

      You give it a model that runs.

      A spreadsheet was never a document. It was a program, zipped into a grid: 951 hand-written formulas, copied out to 52,148. So I built the unzipper: give it a workbook, get back the small program someone wrote, a working copy that gives Excel's answers, without Excel.

      An AI inside Excel changes the open workbook and waits for Excel to recalculate, one question at a time. This copy answers any AI, thousands of times over, and none of them can change it.

      If occupancy lands at 90% instead of 94%, what's the GP IRR? Asked of the unzipped model15.9%GP IRR at 90% occupancy, down from 21.4% · answered in 0.06 s

      The AI talks. The model does the arithmetic.

      September – October 2026

      Translating was the easy part.

      The hard part was trusting it. So Excel became the referee: change the inputs, let Excel recalculate, read all 52,148 formulas back, compare every one.

      0cells compared with Excel 0mismatches 0:00:00of Excel time, played back fast

      Each square is one what-if: new inputs, Excel recalculates, and every one of its 52,148 formulas is checked against the model, to one part in a million. All 582 agree, with nothing set aside, even where Excel's own XIRR finds no ordinary answer. That took 2 hours 30 minutes of Excel; it replays here in seconds.

      The 582: 312 that move one of its 105 key inputs (of the 610 it names) to its low, base or high value, 200 that move five to ten at once, and 70, the darker squares, that move the levers this page asks about: 56 one at a time, 14 together.

      On every one of the 582, the model gave Excel's answer. Whether its assumptions are right is still the underwriter's call. Where the numbers come from ↓

      As a file, it's 48 KB.

      The workbook is 1.2 MB. The model keeps only what turns inputs into answers.

      About the paper's 25 times, by file size. Except this one still runs.

      By formula count it is 55 times fewer: 951 written by hand, against 52,148 in the grid.

      Smaller, and it knows more.

      Excel shows an AI an address and a number: Print Summary!H13 is 0.214.

      The model comes with a map of itself: every input and output by name, its unit, and what feeds what.

      So the AI starts from “GP IRR: 21.4%”. Not from a grid.

      And it goes anywhere.

      Same inputs, same answer, every time. A saved scenario is just its inputs: 63 bytes.

      No Excel needed: on a server, inside an AI skill, offline in a browser tab. The deal stops living on one PC.

      And when someone wants Excel, it writes one: any scenario, every formula live, the same numbers.

      Next: every side of the deal.

      The buyer, the broker, the seller and the lender each keep their own model of the same building, with their own names for the same things.

      Each unzipped model carries its whole context, and an AI can query any of it, any time. Lining four up is the next piece of work.

      For the data people: a semantic layer for underwriting models. ≈ marks names that don't quite mean the same thing.

      You don't read it. You unzip it, and it runs.

      Where the numbers come from
      • The model: a senior-living acquisition model I built so it could be public. Formula counts and Trace Precedents from the workbook itself.
      • Excel: 582 sign-off runs, October 16, 2026, on engine 478797ac, each one changing inputs, recalculating and reading every formula back; 15 s is their median, Excel's side alone. On November 3 the engine that runs this page (da355ba5) gave the same value as 478797ac in every formula of 577 of the 578 of those runs whose inputs the model still accepts, and in all but one formula of the last; that run went through Excel again on November 2, 2026, and matched this engine in every formula. A formula matches when the model is within one millionth of Excel's value, or within 0.000001 where that value is near zero.
      • The other workbooks: each one's latest Excel sign-off record, October 2026. Circular cells match within 0.002.
      • The model's speed and every run in “asking backwards”: engine 009e9709, single-threaded, on a laptop, October 2026.
      • The board: 5,001 runs of the model, one per scenario, twenty assumptions (ten about the building, ten about the deal) each drawn evenly across a range around today's value, inside its verified range. Care staffing is held at today's level in every run.
      • The deal card and the stress matrix: engine runs of the same model, October 2026; the matrix holds staffing too.
